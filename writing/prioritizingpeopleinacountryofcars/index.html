Prioritizing People in a Country of Cars

The United States is a car’s world. The vehicles have managed to wedge their way into crucial parts of nearly everyone’s life, including mine. Once I acquired my driver’s license right after my sixteenth birthday—a major rite of passage in the U.S.—I gained new levels of independence and freedom. Home, school, work, the gym, cello and tennis lessons, and many other places I’m not thinking of—these are all places I drive to and from nearly every week. The next step after the license was the first car—and since I was lucky enough to get mine, I’ve added more than 10,000 miles to the odometer in under a year. Now, I don’t have to constantly be asking for rides, or relying on my parents to get me places. And don’t get me wrong, this newfound independence and freedom is great—but this is where the good news screeches to a halt.

Yes, the United States is a car’s world. But we’re dependent. Seemingly endless miles of roads and highways stretch and pave every which way across the U.S. Cities and towns aren’t planned for people—instead they sprawl for miles with “stroads” that span several lanes, making it harder to get around without a vehicle. This car dependency in cities and towns has a negative mental and physical effect on its inhabitants, with cars spewing greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, causing fewer people-centric cities, and reducing opportunities for people who can’t drive or afford an automobile—and mitigating this dependency would be better for humans and our environment.

To understand how car dependency got America in its grips, one must first jump backwards in time to 1896, the year the automobile was first introduced to the U.S. by Charles and Frank Duryea. The public marveled at the Duryeas’ vehicle—The Chicago Times-Herald described the Duryea car as “an admitted mechanical achievement, highly adapted to some of the most urgent needs of our civilization.”¹ After this success and publicity, the Duryeas founded the U.S.’s first automobile company: the Duryea Motor Wagon Company. Importantly, this is the first company created with the sole purpose of selling cars to the public—1896 is the year that Americans were first given the opportunity to purchase a car, and this is where it seems America’s car obsession began. Then, automobile advancements over the next twelve years led to Henry Ford’s famous 1908 Model T. The Model T was vastly more affordable, easier to drive and repair, and due to improved manufacturing, more widely available to the average consumer.² This ushered in a new era for cars in the U.S., and it was from then on that the automobile began its ascent to its place as a staple of American life. After several decades of steady increases in car purchases in the U.S., the automobile industry halted to support the WWII effort on the home front—but once the war ended and soldiers began to return home, factories started right back up with more and more Americans beginning to demand cars.² In response to this influx in popularity of motor vehicles, highways were funded and constructed in abundance, beginning in the early twentieth century with the Federal Aid Road Act of 1916³ and building to today’s 160,955 miles of U.S. highway seemingly spreading all throughout each urban center and near each small town⁴.

By 1980, 87.2 percent of American households owned at least one motor vehicle, over half owned more than one, and nearly all U.S. car sales were for replacement.² By 1980, there were 710.7 vehicles per thousand people in the U.S.—a number which has only increased to the mid-eight hundreds since then—compared to 1900’s mere 0.1.⁵ As is evident, it only took a few short years—84, using the Duryeas’ company as the starting point and 1980 as the ending point—for Americans to become dependent on cars.

The United States is car-dependent. Great. Why does it matter? Cars do offer an incredible degree of freedom, and there’s nothing inherently wrong with them, right? So what’s the issue?

To start with the most well-known issue, a discussion of cars’ contributions to global warming is necessary. If an average American is asked about the cons cars carry in general, it’s probably a safe bet to guess that they’d talk about global warming or automobile emissions in some way. And this isn’t for no reason—global warming is an extremely pertinent topic in modern life, and it gets more attention every day. Part of this attention is the awareness that cars are spewing tons of carbon dioxide and other harmful greenhouse gases like methane, nitrous oxide, and hydrofluorocarbons.⁶ After all, transportation accounts for a large amount of the United States’ greenhouse gas emissions—according to the Environmental Protection Agency, approximately 28 percent, making it the largest contributing sector.⁶ This figure is one that has only grown over the last few years, and one that will continue to grow if not addressed.⁶ And this is evident in the fact that on-road vehicle emissions have increased steadily over the past several decades—for example, 1,476.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent was ejected into the atmosphere by on-road vehicles in 2022, an entire 22.9 percent increase since 1990.⁷

So, greenhouse gas emissions are one of, if not the biggest and well-known problems when it comes to the negative effects of cars. However, the problem runs deeper than just car emissions. There are other negative effects besides the environment that go unnoticed and live under the surface in the U.S. One of these issues is that cities and towns are more and more being constructed around cars and not around people, which leads to less social connection and more unhappiness. This is a phenomenon described by Muizz Ahktar from Vox: “Americans make most of their friends at work, followed by school, existing friend networks, their neighborhood, their houses of worship, and clubs or organizations. However, the way most American cities, towns, and suburbs have been designed makes it increasingly difficult to access these places and form friendships and communities.”⁸ When our workplaces, schools, neighborhoods and other places are so spread out and sprawling, it becomes impossible to make connections with anyone without getting in a car. This is demonstrated by the fact that, in 2021, more than half of all car trips—about 52 percent—were less than a mere three miles.⁹ We can take a look at my hometown of St. Joseph, Michigan as an example. There’s a nice, little downtown area that’s very walkable, and it’s great for people—but as soon as you go a couple blocks out, it all goes downhill from there. The roads become several lanes wide with concrete and road lines dominating the ground. It becomes a lot more of a challenge to navigate the area as a pedestrian as opposed to a car. This rings true for many students at the high school, including me, who drive there or are driven there often not by choice but due to the limited ways to get to school without a car. The parking lot is seemingly always filled with cars owned by people who live only miles away—but they must drive, as there’s no plausible, convenient alternative to traveling at school when neighborhoods and schools are so spaced out. This doesn’t feel right, this doesn’t feel like how it should be.

This issue brings up another, even more under-the-radar issue—if cars are required in many places to even do something as simple as making connections, what happens to people who can’t drive, or don’t have a car? In an article from The Guardian, Anna Zivarts, who was born with a neurological condition that prevents her from driving, said, “Everything is built around cars… We need to get the voices of those who can’t drive–disabled people, seniors, immigrants, poor folks–into the room because the people making decisions drive everywhere… They don’t know what it’s like to have to spend two hours riding the bus”.¹⁰ The people who can’t drive, due to medical issues, old age, or other obstacles, are forced to stay at home, especially in a sprawling place like St. Joseph. This reminds me of my grandma here in St. Joe—her physical health has declined over the past few years, making it increasingly difficult and uncomfortable for her to drive. But this is strenuous, as everything is so spread out—the grocery store, my family’s house, church, and nearly anywhere else she might go is miles away and only accessible by motor vehicle. So, it’s hard for her to leave the house by herself, with my grandpa usually taking her places if necessary—and what happens when he can’t drive? This hasn’t even considered the people who are not prevented from driving physically, but economically—where they cannot afford a car to reach these workplaces that are sprawled and spaced away from their living areas. In 2013, 6.3 million capable workers were without a personal vehicle, leaving about 4.5 percent of the workforce hindered.¹¹ These 6.3 million or more are not only limited in the sense that they cannot get around without a car, but in the social sense too—when people are constricted to a radius of travel by foot or by public transportation, often infrastructure that’s neglected in American cities and towns, they won’t make as many connections at work, school, places of worship, or other areas. Thus, this car focus in cities and towns causes an opportunity gap and demonstrates the problem Zivarts describes. To solve this, Zivarts advocates for helping these people by, firstly, simply considering them.¹⁰ She asks that when designing or updating cities and towns, consider building them not for cars, but for people so individuals without access to cars have opportunities and make connections without the help of an automobile.¹⁰

There’s a lot to work on when it comes to America’s car dependence. Over a relatively short span of time cars have become necessary to to travel even short distances and make connections for most people in America, when there’s better ways to go about travel. Cars expel greenhouse gases that harm the atmosphere and warm the earth, which in turn harms us. Car centrism spreads cities and towns out to be less people-friendly which causes fewer social connections. These sprawling cities reduce opportunities for people who cannot drive. There’s more ways that cars are harming us than helping us in the state of the country, and that’s why we need to design cities for people, not cars.
