All the Stars Are Planes

Leaning against a door of my dented 2008 Chevy Impala, I tilted my head back to try to get a better view of the night sky. My car and I stood alone in the parking lot, and I was hoping it might happen to be one of those good nights where I could spot a few stars or maybe even some constellations. I’m no astronomer, but this aspiration turned out to be too auspicious—for every time I glimpsed a luminous pinprick far above, it ended up being not a star, but an airplane. After this happened once, I thought the event may have been a fluke. But then, it happened again—I spotted not a star but another plane crossing the night sky. And again. The sky was filled not with beautiful stars and celestial constellations as I had hoped, but with earthbound planes. All the stars were planes.

I had come up short in my skywide search for something majestic in its permanence, something awe-inspiring in its unrelenting place in the universe. Does something so ceaseless even exist? I wondered. So, I looked at my own place in the universe. What remains permanent in my life and mind? What remains unrelenting?

For one, I am a relentless reader. In fact, I’ve been listening to and absorbing stories since before I even could read. And the first thing I ever did read out in the real world—I remember this exact moment—was one of those hanging Meijer aisle signs. Unremarkable in its content but fully remarkable in what it brought—who knew that one grocery sign could open the whole world to me? Since I’ve moved on from grocery signs, from “cereal” and “salty snacks”, and from the ever-enduring literary classics of my middle school years (Dog Man, Harry Potter, et cetera), I’ve started to read pretty much anything I cross paths with that can bring me new information about the world and new perspectives that I didn’t previously consider. I read articles and research, like on how car dependency can arise and how it can increase the opportunity gap, or on how rugged individualism mindset induced a decline in Americans’ empathy—both topics shaping the way I live life in my country and what I can do to change things I care about. I branch out, like when I read the stream of consciousness novel Septology by Nobel winner Jon Fosse, challenging myself to think deeply about my ideas regarding religion and art. Or in Fernanda Melchor’s Hurricane Season, I read stories of how generational abuse and trauma cycles in rural Mexico—a topic so far outside my comfort zone, but a topic I’m so glad to have stumbled across, simply because it provides me with new knowledge about serious global issues and the people of the world I live in.

Considering these new perspectives leads me towards a maintained sense of gratefulness. For example, I recently crashed my car attempting to avoid an animal on the road. I was incredibly lucky—after swerving around it, my Chevy landed on its side in soft, springy dirt and foliage. But had I crashed even five seconds earlier, the car would’ve instead wrapped clean around a telephone pole. My life could’ve been altered instantly—I could’ve been permanently paralyzed, or very possibly killed. Upon reflecting on this incident, I zoomed out. I utilized my perspectives and empathy and realized, life-altering events like car crashes and much worse already happen to countless people across the globe every day, and my dented Impala reminds me that I’m not exempt.

So, I zoom out and remain grateful. I remain grateful and I remain reading and I remain gaining new perspectives. These are the values that I hold close to my heart and mind, so that even if my sky is full of planes I can depend on at least a few stars to guide me.
