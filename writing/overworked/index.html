Overworked

Racing through the St. Joseph streets in my sedan, I do the math in my head. 4:00 is when I’m scheduled for work, and I planned to leave tennis practice early to get there on time—but as I’m leaving the courts, my watch reads 3:55. I was going to be late. After a couple minutes of frantic driving, I switched on the turn signal—then I would be only a couple minutes away—but by the time I pulled into the parking lot at work, the pixelated digital clock on the dashboard glowed 4:15. I was 15 minutes late.

Well, what should I have expected after packing my schedule to the brim? I was bound to be late to something after attempting to attend not just work and tennis practices, but also National Honors Society sessions, robotics meetings, cello and tennis lessons, four different orchestra’s rehearsals and concerts, gatherings for several clubs… and that’s not even all. I remember a time, not even that long ago, where most weeks I had at least a couple days to come home and relax. Nowadays, it seems my calendar is overflowing and the only rest I get is from midnight to six a.m. each day—just to wake up and do it all over again.

Don’t get me wrong. I signed up for all of these clubs. I'm driving myself to each meeting, rehearsal, practice, and concert. I’m surely not the only high schooler that signs up for tons of extracurriculars, but why fill every day with clubs and events? Why leave for school at seven in the morning and get home at ten thirty each day? Maybe it’s because these events are interesting or fulfilling, like, in my case, the orchestras. Or maybe money’s the motivator. But, at the root of it, it seems that there is an underlying idealization of being busy that’s contaminating my schedule. America encourages me to work as much as I can, be as productive as I can. Its promises of success in life and college echo in my ears, the promises of more cash—but only if I work hard enough. And if I don’t, if I take time for myself, I’m guilty of wasting time. So, I subconsciously pack my schedule, no matter the burnout and anxiety that I and others experience across the country. No matter the weary midnight homework after hours of school and work, that I complete with my eyes drooping. It’s all fine, because I know this will help me succeed, right?

This glorification of overwork is a large-scale, societal issue, so it’s going to be difficult to change it all. But, at an individual level, I can and should make time for myself. Success can’t consist of work alone: cutting out free time, relaxing once in a while, taking a nap—these are all a huge part of being well-rounded, happy and successful. Embracing free time might take some getting used to, but trust me: it’ll pay off.
