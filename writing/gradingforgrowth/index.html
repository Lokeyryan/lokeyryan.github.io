Grading for Growth

“Four minutes left,” my math teacher called out during my math class’ big chapter test. Looking up at the clock in panic, I realized that the hour was almost over–and there were still three out of 16 problems to go. After feverishly scribbling down my final answer for the problem I was just working on, I started on the next one, hoping that maybe I could get partial credit for an attempt–but I knew it was futile. I was going to have to accept getting a bad grade on this–bringing me down to a C or so in math.

This was one of the times that I found myself thinking far more about my performance on a test and my grade than the actual content of the class and my interest in the topic—and it’s not just me: another time this happened was in my mom’s fifth grade classroom. She had assigned a small writing task to complete—her class was to write a short essay entailing something they cared about. Shortly after this was assigned, a student approached her and asked, “Is this for a grade?” She remembers her response being almost incredulous–based on the question, she challenged her entire class to reconsider the focus of classwork, and reminded them that she wouldn’t grade everything, but she would use everything as data to drive instruction that would focus on growth for all students collectively. She aimed to take the focus from the “is this a grade” question to growth and focus on the process of learning.

There are other forms of assessment than traditional test-based learning—for example, my math teacher in middle school: Mr. H., who used a much more growth-based assessment system. While we learned and did activities, we would write our reflections on the lesson, what we learned, and vocabulary for each lesson—then, at the end of the unit, we would make a portfolio based on all of these reflections. Mr. H. would grade us primarily on this portfolio, which mirrored our understanding of the topics, comprehension, and how much we were paying attention–interestingly enough, the exact same thing a test does, but in a much more open and constructive way. This improved our ability to analyze information, connect ideas, reflect, develop critical thinking skills, and create, rather than showing that we can mindlessly regurgitate information given to us without even thinking deeply about the topic, like a test does. We were still graded on the portfolio–but does that really matter? The process of performing the activities and creating the portfolio were already better reflections of the extent of the student’s learning abilities. Nevertheless, we did take tests, but we were allowed to use our portfolios on them, which just shows how much more of a priority the portfolio held over the test when assessing the student’s learning and growth over the course of the unit.

According to a research paper by Jeffery Schinske and Kimberly Tanner, from De Anza College and San Francisco State University respectively, “Because feedback traditionally has been given by the instructor and transmitted to students, Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (educational researchers referred to in the study) argue that students have been deprived of opportunities to become self-regulated learners who can detect their own errors in thinking. They advocate for incorporating techniques such as self-reflection and student dialogue into the assessment process.”¹ Mr. H. did this as well—he focused on reflection during instruction, incorporating peer and self-reflection, summarization, error analysis, and revision as important components into his math class’ everyday work—reflection boosts deep thinking and helps students understand topics at a higher level.

Traditional test-based learning doesn’t focus enough on the reflection, growth, or the process of learning. Even if it wasn’t intended to be this way, it gives a sense of impartiality about how the students are actually doing in school and in each class. There are many negative effects to this teaching style as well, and I can speak from personal experience; during times of study for high-stakes tests, I have a period of time before each test where I stay up until far too late, studying for the test–resulting in loss of sleep, which in turn leads to anxiety and tiredness, and eventually panic and excessive nervousness during the test. According to the same Schinske-Tanner study, “Grades can dampen existing intrinsic motivation, give rise to extrinsic motivation, enhance fear of failure, reduce interest, decrease enjoyment in class work, increase anxiety, hamper performance on follow-up tasks, stimulate avoidance of challenging tasks, and heighten competitiveness.” High-stakes tests, and, in turn, their grades, force most students to think about their performance and motivates them based on fear of failure—among other negative consequences that will follow students throughout their lives—instead of how they can improve important life skills and how they can understand the content of the class better, which can lead to anxiety and more negative effects.

Despite all this, tests aren’t ineffective at what they’re designed to do. There are two main types of tests—formative assessments for one, which gauges students’ understanding of topics being taught so that the teacher can tweak instruction to better help the students. It can range from the teacher asking for feedback on a lesson to a small test. The other type is summative assessments, which measures mastery of content learned. Both of these types of assessments are also supposed to communicate to students how they are doing in class. And, well, these assessments do their job. But—traditional test-based learning only captures a student’s understanding in the allotted test time, not to mention that different students work at different speeds. Traditional test-based learning focuses less on progress and the process of learning whereas growth-based learning encourages students to be curious and think deeply about topics, which can follow them throughout the rest of their education and life. Despite this, there does need to be a good balance between growth-based learning and traditional testing and assessment.

However, something new is in order here. Maybe a system that uses an accumulative portfolio, like Mr. H. did. Maybe a more growth-based system that incorporates more hands-on learning to increase student interest, grow the students’ knowledge, and encourage curiosity and deeper thinking. Maybe a system that focuses less on the tests, quizzes, and grades, and more on the process of learning. Maybe a system that uses different kinds of reflection, such as self and peer evaluation, summarization, and error analysis as opposed to blatant criticism represented by a single percentage. Maybe a system that fosters a genuine interest in learning that will follow the students throughout their lives. Too much assessment-based teaching–and the grades that come with it–has consequences that outweigh its benefits. All of the negative results of too much assessment-based grading calls for a much-needed change in ways–and something should be done about it.
