Our Own Echo Chambers

I make my way to the door after I hear the rapping of knuckles on my family’s front door. Standing on the porch is my uncle, who lives in Chicago, coming to visit over Thanksgiving break as he usually does. We welcome him in, and begin to talk; How are the Lions doing? What have my brother and I been up to? It’s nice, to converse with him over dinner; we only get to see him once or twice a year.

But then, after dinner, things start to go downhill. My parents’ and uncle’s topic of conversation veers into the political sphere and it gradually starts to get louder, as if their dialogue was some abstract vocal piece of music marked with a hidden crescendo.

This is usually when my brother and I have to retreat. Maybe it’s to our rooms, maybe downstairs—just somewhere with closed doors. These walls and doors between us and the arguments absorb some of the sound, but not much; we still hear the rapid back and forth, the constant interruptions.

They’ll argue about the economy—it’s in dire decline, my uncle might say. No, it’s not, my parents rebut, and each party defends their position. Next, it’s that activist protests have become violent riots and someone needs to control them; gun control isn’t strict enough, and kids are dying because of it; climate change isn’t that serious of an issue… Take your pick of a controversial political topic, and they’ll probably feud over it.

Over the last few years, it seems like it’s become harder and harder for my parents and my uncle to get along. Nearly every meeting seems to end in one of these polarized arguments about countless political issues, and the argument is hardly ever resolved. Enduring shouts from both sides, sometimes late into the night, my brother and I just linger like condensation on a can. We’re droplets slowly sliding down the aluminum, unsure of how to distract ourselves from the verbal onslaughts—with no choice but to stand on the sidelines and listen to the relentless voices.

The confusing part: I know that everyone involved is an empathetic, kind human being at heart—my uncle, and both my parents. In fact, my uncle and my mom are siblings; they’ve shared a childhood. So, what went wrong? Why do they fight so frequently and so fervently?

What’s most likely a major contributing factor as to why it’s becoming harder to get along politically is the silently growing presence of echo chambers across everyone’s digital lives—according to a study from the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, echo chambers “limit the exposure to diverse perspectives and favor the formation of groups of like-minded users framing and reinforcing a shared narrative.”¹ These echo chambers form as a result of artificial intelligence algorithms used by countless sites—“Netflix and YouTube use them to push you new shows and videos; Google and Facebook use them to rank the content in your search results and news feed,” as MIT Technology Reviewexplains.²

Basically, when you or I scroll YouTube, skim our Facebook feeds, or browse Twitter, these AI-fueled algorithms are hard at work to provide you and me with content that identifies with our likes and opinions the most. Nearly every one of these social media sites I’m mentioning—Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and others like TikTok or Reddit—has become a major new breeding ground where these AI-driven algorithms can form and thrive. And while this is happening, echo chambers are forming, digging us deeper into a hole of confirmation bias—and this effect is especially potent when it comes to politics.

Algorithms are diligently forming and developing these echo chambers for my uncle when he listens to podcasts or watches videos for hours each morning on various sites, and for my parents when they scroll Facebook or Twitter before bed.

Then, when different family members are exposed to vastly different echo chambers, relationships go bad. They become strained. Argumentative. At times disrespectful, like in the case of my parents and my uncle, even when every one of them is worthy of each others’ respect, no matter the conversation topic. It becomes increasingly difficult for them to have a decent debate when we are all sinking further and further into our own echo chambers.

When this happens, when we’re falling down and down these echo chambers like Alice on her way to Wonderland, we are blind. We are led to question information that aligns with our beliefs less. We are led not to take alternative viewpoints at face value. As the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences mentions in their study, this polarization resulting from echo chambers thereby results in easier spread of misinformation and disinformation, which can cause critical issues not just between family members but between entire societal groups—as illustrated by the immense division across America that was present leading up to the recent 2024 Presidential election, and continues to be present today.¹ Yes, I believe echo chambers have a lot to do with how separated we’ve become as a nation.

And really, it’s no one’s fault. None of us want to succumb to AI algorithms. MIT Technology Review claims “users will tolerate” algorithms that are less cunning and don’t reflect their views so directly, which would make echo chambers significantly more difficult to form.² However, it’s hard not to fall for these algorithms when they are seemingly omnipresent across the internet, and it’s hard to recognize that it’s even happening.

For example, my grandparents have fallen down the echo chamber that is Fox News. Fox News takes advantage of people more susceptible to echo chambers, which in many cases is older people who are less familiar with the digital realm—according to The Michigan Daily, “of Americans 65 and older, 37% say Fox is their main source of political news.”³

This adds up, and all of a sudden we’re fighting at the dinner table. It’s uncomfortable, but the first step in ending these fights is to recognize the effect these algorithms have on you and dig yourself out of your echo chamber—this will lead to more empathy, more open-mindedness, and maybe, a better future for us all.
